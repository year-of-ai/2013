**Key figures**: Malala Yousafzai, Ziauddin Yousafzai (father and co-founder), Ban Ki-moon (UN Secretary-General), Kailash Satyarthi (co-Nobel laureate, 2014)

## Summary

In March 2013, Malala Yousafzai resumed her education in Birmingham after recovering from a Taliban shooting in October 2012 that had left her gravely injured. Despite her trauma, she emerged as a resolute advocate for girls' education. That same year, at age 16, Malala and her father co-founded the **Malala Fund**, a nonprofit organization dedicated to expanding educational access for girls globally. The fund worked to combat gender-based barriers to learning, supporting programs across Pakistan, Afghanistan, Nigeria, India, Brazil, Ethiopia, Turkey, and Lebanon. TIME magazine named Malala one of the 100 most influential people in the world in 2013, and she became the youngest-ever nominee for the Nobel Peace Prize.

## Early Life and Advocacy in Pakistan

Malala Yousafzai was born on **July 12, 1997**, in Mingora, Swat District, in the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province of northwestern Pakistan. Her father, Ziauddin Yousafzai, ran a chain of schools called the Khushal Public School. Unlike many Pakistani fathers of his era and region, Ziauddin actively encouraged his daughter's education and public voice, entering her name in the local birth register alongside the men of the household — a significant act in a culture where daughters were often unregistered.

From **January to March 2009**, at age 11, Malala wrote an anonymous diary for BBC Urdu under the pseudonym "Gul Makai," describing daily life in the Swat Valley under Taliban occupation. The Taliban had banned girls' education in Swat beginning January 2009, closing or destroying over **400 schools** in the district. The blog, later adapted by The New York Times into a documentary short, *Class Dismissed* (2009), attracted international attention to the education crisis in northwestern Pakistan. In 2011, Malala received Pakistan's first **National Youth Peace Prize** (later renamed the National Malala Peace Prize). Archbishop Desmond Tutu nominated her for the International Children's Peace Prize the same year.

## Taliban Assassination Attempt: October 9, 2012

On **October 9, 2012**, Malala was traveling home from school by bus in Mingora when a Taliban gunman boarded the vehicle, demanded to know which student was Malala Yousafzai, and shot her in the head at close range. Two classmates — Kainat Riaz and Shazia Ramzan — were also wounded in the attack. The assassination was ordered by Taliban commander **Mullah Fazlullah** in direct response to Malala's persistent public advocacy for girls' education and her criticism of the Taliban.

Malala was airlifted to the Army Public School Intensive Care Unit in Peshawar, then transferred to a military hospital in Rawalpindi. On **October 15, 2012**, she was flown to the **Queen Elizabeth Hospital Birmingham** in the United Kingdom — a facility specializing in complex trauma, known particularly for treating military personnel with severe head injuries — where she underwent multiple surgeries. Surgeons removed skull fragments, reconstructed part of her skull with a **titanium plate**, and installed a **cochlear implant** to address nerve damage affecting her hearing. She emerged from a medically induced coma in late October 2012 and began an extended rehabilitation program in Birmingham.

## 2013: Return to School and the UN Birthday Speech

On **March 19, 2013**, Malala began attending **Edgbaston High School for Girls** in Birmingham, marking her first day of formal education since the attack five months earlier. Global media coverage of her return framed it as an act of defiance — the teenager who had been shot for demanding access to school was walking back into a classroom.

The most significant public moment of 2013 came on **July 12, 2013** — her 16th birthday — when Malala addressed the **United Nations Youth Assembly** in New York in an event designated "Malala Day" by UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon. Approximately **1,000 young leaders** and dignitaries attended. Malala delivered a roughly 20-minute address without notes, wearing a shawl given to her by Pakistan's late Prime Minister Benazir Bhutto and a white headscarf as deliberate symbols of Pakistani identity:

> "One child, one teacher, one book, one pen can change the world. Education is the only solution. Education first."

The speech was live-streamed globally and became the most widely shared activist address of the year. Its distribution across social media — the same digital platforms that in 2013 had elevated the [selfie]({{ '/news/arts-culture/selfie-oxford-word-of-year/' | relative_url }}) as a cultural phenomenon and amplified the [Snowden NSA disclosures]({{ '/news/history-politics/snowden-nsa-revelations/' | relative_url }}) — demonstrated how the internet could accelerate both individual self-expression and collective political advocacy simultaneously.

Later that year, on **October 8, 2013** — the eve of the first anniversary of the assassination attempt — Malala's memoir *I Am Malala*, co-written with journalist Christina Lamb, was published simultaneously in the United Kingdom and United States. The book immediately entered bestseller lists in both countries.

## Malala Fund: Mission and Programs

Malala and her father formally co-founded the **Malala Fund** as a nonprofit organization in 2013, incorporated in the United States. The fund's stated mission is to ensure **12 years of free, quality education** for every girl. It operates through three primary strategies:

1. **Advocacy**: Pressuring governments to increase education budgets and eliminate legal and cultural barriers to girls' schooling
2. **Grantmaking**: Funding local educators and community organizations — called "Education Champions" — operating on the ground in countries with the greatest gender gaps in education
3. **Amplification**: Using Malala's global platform to increase public and political attention to girls' denied access to schooling

In its first years of operation, the Malala Fund established programs in Pakistan, Afghanistan, Nigeria, India, Brazil, Ethiopia, Turkey, and Lebanon. The fund's early research documented that more than **130 million girls** were out of school globally, disproportionately concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Its grantmaking focused particularly on secondary school access — the critical inflection point at which many girls in low-income countries exit formal education due to early marriage, domestic labor obligations, or cost barriers. World Bank data cited by the fund estimated that each additional year of secondary schooling for a girl in a low-income country increases her lifetime earnings by approximately **25%**.

## Nobel Peace Prize: Nomination in 2013, Award in 2014

In 2013, Malala became the **youngest person in history to be nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize**. Although the 2013 prize was awarded to the Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons, her nomination signaled a global consensus about the impact of her advocacy at an age when most laureates had not yet begun careers.

On **October 10, 2014**, Malala won the Nobel Peace Prize jointly with Indian children's rights activist **Kailash Satyarthi**, becoming at age 17 the **youngest Nobel laureate in any category in the prize's history**. The Norwegian Nobel Committee cited her "struggle against the suppression of children and young people and for the right of all children to education." The joint award with Satyarthi was widely interpreted as a statement that the rights of children across South Asia required cross-religious and cross-border solidarity. Malala directed her share of the approximately **$1.1 million Nobel prize** toward the construction of a secondary school for Palestinian refugee girls in Lebanon, operated in partnership with the United Nations Relief and Works Agency (UNRWA).

## Significance

Malala's emergence as a global public advocate in 2013 marked a turning point in international discussions about girls' education and the right to learn in conflict zones. The founding of the Malala Fund institutionalized her activism, creating a durable organization able to channel resources and international attention toward educational equality beyond her own biography. Her status as both a survivor and campaigner — speaking from lived experience rather than institutional authority — amplified her message across audiences that conventional development organizations had rarely reached.

Her 2013 trajectory also illustrated the dual nature of the digital platforms dominating that year's cultural landscape: the same infrastructure that gave rise to the selfie, to Bitcoin speculation, and to the exposure of mass surveillance could also carry the recorded testimony of a 15-year-old girl to a global audience of millions. The medium was not the message in 2013 — but it was the amplifier.
