## Summary

On May 10, 2013, director Baz Luhrmann's film adaptation of F. Scott Fitzgerald's 1925 novel *The Great Gatsby* opened in wide theatrical release across North America, marking a significant cultural event in American cinema. The film had held its world premiere nine days earlier, on May 1, 2013, at Lincoln Center in New York City, and it went on to open the 66th Cannes Film Festival on May 15, 2013. The film starred Leonardo DiCaprio as Jay Gatsby, Carey Mulligan as Daisy Buchanan, and Joel Edgerton as Tom Buchanan. Shot primarily in Australia and on elaborate soundstage sets, the production carried an estimated budget of $105 million—making it one of the most expensive films of the year.

Luhrmann's rendition represented the fourth major cinematic adaptation of Fitzgerald's novel. Previous film versions had been released in 1926, 1949, and 1974 (with Robert Redford and Mia Farrow). The 2013 adaptation departed substantially from its predecessors in visual style, favoring a maximalist, expressionistic aesthetic that emphasized the decadence, excess, and theatrical performance of Gatsby's Long Island world. Luhrmann employed elaborate costume design, vibrant color palettes, rapid editing, and an eclectic soundtrack blending 1920s jazz with contemporary hip-hop to create what reviewers described as an immersive sensory experience.

DiCaprio's portrayal of Gatsby emphasized the character's enigmatic romanticism and underlying desperation. Mulligan's Daisy emerged as a more sympathetically drawn character than in some earlier adaptations, emphasizing her internal conflict and complicity in the novel's moral economy. The supporting cast included actors Isla Fisher, Jason Clarke, Callan McAuliffe, and Elizabeth Debicki. The production design by Catherine Martin won widespread critical acclaim for its meticulous recreation of 1920s luxury architecture and interior design, though the overall aesthetic leaned toward stylized artificiality rather than historical verisimilitude.

## Background and Development

The rights to *The Great Gatsby* have been contested property throughout the twentieth century. Fitzgerald published the novel in 1925 to initial modest commercial success and critical recognition; it did not achieve canonical status in American letters until after his death in 1940. Throughout the mid-20th and late-20th centuries, the novel's themes of wealth, desire, the American Dream's bankruptcy, and class mobility made it a subject of recurring literary and cinematic interpretation.

Baz Luhrmann had long expressed interest in adapting *Gatsby*, and his prior films—including *Moulin Rouge!* (2001)—demonstrated his affinity for visual excess, theatrical composition, and eclectic musical accompaniment. Studio executives and producers had debated the novel's cinematability for decades; the book's interiority, its reliance on Nick Carraway's retrospective narration, and its elliptical narrative structure posed challenges for linear film storytelling. Luhrmann's solution emphasized spectacle, visual metaphor, and non-diegetic music to externalize Gatsby's inner life and the moral atmosphere of the Jazz Age.

## Critical Reception and Commercial Performance

The 2013 film received mixed-to-positive critical reviews. Critics praised the production design, costume work, and DiCaprio's performance, though reactions to Luhrmann's maximalist visual style divided audiences. Some reviewers celebrated the film's fidelity to the novel's emotional core and its defense of the Jazz Age world against contemporary judgment; others contended that visual excess overshadowed narrative subtlety. The film's cumulative box-office performance exceeded $353 million worldwide, making it one of 2013's commercially successful films, though reviews were mixed on whether the film adequately honored Fitzgerald's literary achievement.

Carey Mulligan's portrayal of Daisy sparked particular critical discussion. Some viewers and critics found her interpretation more sympathetic and complex than previous film Daisies, suggesting depths of character awareness and agency that earlier adaptations had underplayed. Others questioned whether Luhrmann's visual language inadvertently undermined the novel's critique of wealth and beauty by aestheticizing both beyond moral judgment.

## Production Design and Visual Language

Catherine Martin's production design and costume direction drew heavily from archival photographs, museum collections, and historical documentation of 1920s American elite culture. The sets recreated Gatsby's mansion, Daisy and Tom's East Egg estate, and the Plaza Hotel (site of the novel's crucial confrontation) with meticulous attention to architectural and decorative detail. The film's use of color—particularly the lavish greens of Gatsby's grounds, the golden tones of party sequences, and the bleached whites of Daisy's drawing room—created a visual language that reinforced thematic meanings around desire, aspiration, and emptiness.

The costume design similarly emphasized excess and historical reference. Women's beaded evening wear, men's tailored suits and fedoras, and accessories including long strings of pearls and jeweled hair pieces created an immersive 1920s aesthetic. The film's attention to material culture—automobiles, china, glass stemware, musical instruments—reinforced the novel's preoccupation with wealth's visible manifestations and the gap between external display and internal authenticity.

## Musical Score and Soundtrack

Luhrmann's compositional approach to music merged period jazz standards with contemporary hip-hop and electronic production. The score, composed by Craig Armstrong, drew on 1920s jazz idioms while incorporating orchestral arrangements that amplified the emotional stakes of key scenes. The soundtrack featured artists including Jay-Z (who served as a creative consultant), Beyoncé, Fergie, and others, creating a deliberate anachronism that invited audiences to experience the Jazz Age through a contemporary sonic lens.

Critics debated the effectiveness of this approach. Some argued that the hip-hop elements created unintended comedy or undermined the period setting. Others contended that the anachronistic soundtrack captured something essential about how the past is always interpreted through the lens of the present, making the stylistic collision itself thematically resonant with the novel's preoccupation with nostalgia, aspiration, and the impossibility of recapturing the past.

## Significance

The 2013 *Great Gatsby* adaptation emerged during a period of renewed American fascination with the 1920s aesthetic. Vintage fashion, speakeasy bars, and Jazz Age-themed events had become cultural phenomena in the early 2010s, particularly among urban millennials seeking to reconcile contemporary global capitalism with a romanticized earlier modernity. Luhrmann's film capitalized on and reinforced this aesthetic nostalgia while simultaneously offering a moral argument that the Jazz Age's glamour masked spiritual and ethical bankruptcy.

The film also illustrated broader 2013 cultural preoccupations with wealth inequality, the financial system's instability, and the American Dream's contested meanings in a post-2008-financial-crisis era. Gatsby's obsessive pursuit of Daisy as the embodiment of wealth and status resonated with contemporary anxieties about class, consumption, and whether material accumulation could deliver promised happiness. The film's commercial and critical success suggested that Fitzgerald's century-old critique of American aspiration retained powerful contemporary relevance.

Additionally, DiCaprio's star power and the film's visual spectacle made it a significant media event in 2013, dominating cultural conversation about cinema's role in interpreting literary classics and about the relationship between theatrical excess and narrative substance.

## Awards and Academy Recognition

At the 86th Academy Awards ceremony held on March 2, 2014, the film was nominated in two categories and won both: **Best Production Design** (Catherine Martin, with set decorator Beverley Dunn) and **Best Costume Design** (Catherine Martin). The wins were widely interpreted as recognition of the film's extraordinary material achievement in recreating 1920s luxury aesthetics, regardless of questions about narrative depth. They were not Catherine Martin's first: she had previously won the same two Oscars for Luhrmann's *Moulin Rouge!* at the 74th Academy Awards in 2002, and the *Gatsby* victories made her the most-awarded Australian in Oscar history to that point.

Lana Del Rey's "Young and Beautiful," a ballad written for the film and performed during the climactic Plaza Hotel confrontation, was not among the film's Academy Award nominations but became one of the most recognized songs associated with the production, charting internationally and earning the singer widespread critical attention.

## 2013 Cultural Context

The film's May 2013 release coincided with a broader cultural fascination in the early 2010s with retro aesthetics, institutional critique, and spectacle. Earlier in 2013, the [Harlem Shake viral phenomenon](/news/arts-culture/harlem-shake-viral-phenomenon/) illustrated a public appetite for participatory excess and internet-mediated cultural performance—a dynamic not entirely unlike the mass spectacle of Gatsby's parties. Later in the year, the designation of "selfie" as Oxford Dictionaries' Word of the Year reflected the same preoccupation with curated self-presentation and image management that Fitzgerald's novel had diagnosed nearly a century earlier in Gatsby's carefully constructed persona.

The film's post-financial-crisis resonance was widely noted by critics. The 2008 global financial crisis and its aftermath—characterized by bank bailouts, growing wealth concentration, and popular anger at elite impunity—had made the Jazz Age's wealth and excess newly legible as critique rather than aspiration. Gatsby's acquisition of wealth through legally questionable means and his ultimate destruction despite his social ambitions mapped readily onto post-2008 narratives about financial systems and their human costs.
